\DIFdelend \section*{Outline and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Motivation}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{motivation}\DIFaddend }
In this final lecture on seismology we consider the free oscillations of the Earth in further detail. Last time we established the 
general theory, and saw how eigenfrequencies can be observed from the Fourier transforms of sufficiently long seismograms. Today we 
first consider the eigenvalue problem in a \textbf{spherically symmetric earth model} for which the eigenfunctions can be calculated 
with relative ease. An important point is that the eigenspaces in a spherically symmetric model are \textbf{degenerate}, each having 
dimension $2l+1$ for some integer degree $l\ge0$. This material depends on the properties of \textbf{vector spherical harmonics}, but 
you are not expected to \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{know }\DIFaddend the details. We then show using \textbf{first-order perturbation theory} how in the real Earth this degeneracy 
is \textbf{split by rotation and lateral heterogeneity}. To conclude, an overview is given of radial and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{laterally }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{lateral }\DIFaddend variations of density 
within the Earth, with some current research questions highlighted.

\section*{Vector spherical harmonics}
To discuss the eigenfunctions of a spherically symmetric earth model we need to review \textbf{spherical harmonic expansions}
 of scalar and vector functions. The emphasis here is only on establishing notations that will be useful later. You will 
 \textbf{not be expected to carry out any detailed calculations with either scalar or vector spherical harmonics}.

Using spherical polar co-ordinates $(r, \theta, \varphi)$ the gradient operator is given by
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\nabla=\hat{\mathbf{r}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi},
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:1}
\nabla=\hat{\mathbf{r}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r}\hat{\bm{\theta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\hat{\bm{\varphi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi},
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where, for example, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is a unit vector in the radial direction. This expression can be conveniently written
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\nabla=\hat{\mathbf{r}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r}\nabla_{1},
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:2}
\nabla=\hat{\mathbf{r}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r}\nabla_{1},
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
which acts to define the \textbf{tangential gradient operator}, $\nabla_{1}$, for functions defined on the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{unit-sphere}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{unit sphere}\DIFaddend , $\mathbb{S}^{2}$. 
In terms of $\nabla_{1}$ we can define related operators $\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\nabla_{1}$ and $\nabla_{1}^{2}$. Up to suitable \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{scale-factors}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{scale factors}\DIFaddend , the first
 is the quantum mechanical \textbf{angular momentum operator}, $\hat{\mathbf{L}}$, and the second the \textbf{total squared angular momentum}, $\hat{L}^{2}$.

Spherical harmonic functions are simultaneous eigenfunctions for the 
 \textbf{commuting} and \textbf{self-adjoint operators} $\nabla_{1}^{2}$ and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\frac{1}{\ii}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}$}\DIFaddend , the latter being proportional to the \textbf{\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{z-component}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$z$-component}\DIFaddend }
  of $\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\nabla_{1}$. The defining relations are
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\nabla_{1}^{2}Y_{lm}=-l(l+1)Y_{lm}, \quad \frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial Y_{lm}}{\partial\varphi}=mY_{lm},
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:3}
\nabla_{1}^{2}Y_{lm}=-l(l+1)Y_{lm}, \quad \frac{1}{\ii}\frac{\partial Y_{lm}}{\partial\varphi}=mY_{lm},
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where $l$ is the \textbf{degree} of the spherical harmonic which takes non-negative integer values, while for fixed $l$ the \textbf{order}, $m$, 
is an integer such that $-l\le m\le l$. Note that the eigenspace of $\nabla_{1}^{2}$ with degree \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{l }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$l$ }\DIFaddend is $(2l+1)$-fold degenerate, 
this being characteristic of the eigenspaces of \textbf{rotationally invariant operators}. The spherical harmonics satisfy the orthonormality relation
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{lm}^{*}Y_{l'm'}\dd S=\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}.
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:4}
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{lm}^{*}Y_{l'm'}\dd S=\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}.
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
They also form a \textbf{complete basis} for suitably regular \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{function }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{functions }\DIFaddend $f$ on $\mathbb{S}^{2}$, and hence we
 can form the spherical harmonic expansion
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{f=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}f_{lm}Y_{lm},
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:5}
f=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}f_{lm}Y_{lm},
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where the expansion coefficients are
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{f_{lm}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{lm}^{*}f\dd S.
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:6}
f_{lm}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{lm}^{*}f\dd S.
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
Usually we will write such spherical harmonic expansions simply as $\sum_{lm}f_{lm}Y_{lm}$ leaving implicit the range of the degree and order. 


To work with vector fields it is convenient to introduce the \textbf{vector spherical harmonics} which are defined by
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\mathbf{A}_{lm}=Y_{lm}\hat{\mathbf{r}}, \quad \mathbf{B}_{lm}=\nabla_{1}Y_{lm}, \quad \mathbf{C}_{lm}=-\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\nabla_{1}Y_{lm}.
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:7}
\mathbf{A}_{lm}=Y_{lm}\hat{\mathbf{r}}, \quad \mathbf{B}_{lm}=\nabla_{1}Y_{lm}, \quad \mathbf{C}_{lm}=-\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\nabla_{1}Y_{lm}.
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
The following orthogonality relations can be established\DIFdelbegin \begin{eqnarray*}
\DIFdel{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbf{A}_{l'm'}^{*}\cdot \mathbf{A}_{lm}dS}&\DIFdel{=\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}, }\\
\DIFdel{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbf{B}_{l'm'}^{*}\cdot \mathbf{B}_{lm}dS}&\DIFdel{=l(l+1)\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}, }\\
\DIFdel{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbf{C}_{l'm'}^{*}\cdot \mathbf{C}_{lm}dS}&\DIFdel{=l(l+1)\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'},
}\end{eqnarray*}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{:
}\begin{align}
\DIFadd{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbf{A}_{l'm'}^{*}\cdot \mathbf{A}_{lm}\dd S}&\DIFadd{=\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'},\label{eq:8}}\\
\DIFadd{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbf{B}_{l'm'}^{*}\cdot \mathbf{B}_{lm}\dd S}&\DIFadd{=l(l+1)\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'},\label{eq:9}}\\
\DIFadd{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbf{C}_{l'm'}^{*}\cdot \mathbf{C}_{lm}\dd S}&\DIFadd{=l(l+1)\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'},\label{eq:10}
}\end{align}\DIFaddend 
where $\cdot$ denotes the usual real inner product $\mathbf{a}\cdot \mathbf{b}=a_{i}b_{i}$ for vectors. 
Note that $\mathbf{B}_{lm}$ and $\mathbf{C}_{lm}$ could be scaled such that these functions are orthonormal, but this is not necessary. 
It can be shown that the vector spherical harmonics are complete for vector fields defined on $\mathbb{S}^{2}$, this meaning 
that such a $\mathbf{u}$ can be expanded as
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\mathbf{u}=\sum_{lm}(U_{lm}\mathbf{A}_{lm}+V_{lm}\mathbf{B}_{lm}+W_{lm}\mathbf{C}_{lm}) ,
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:11}
\mathbf{u}=\sum_{lm}(U_{lm}\mathbf{A}_{lm}+V_{lm}\mathbf{B}_{lm}+W_{lm}\mathbf{C}_{lm}),
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
with coefficients $U_{lm}$, $V_{lm}$ and $W_{lm}$ that can be expressed in terms of $\mathbf{u}$ using the above orthogonality relations.

\subsection*{Spheroidal and toroidal modes}
In a non-rotating earth model, we recall that the eigenvalue problem takes the form
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{-\omega^{2}\langle \mathbf{w}|P|\mathbf{s}\rangle+\langle \mathbf{w}|H|\mathbf{s}\rangle=0
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:12}
-\omega^{2}\langle \mathbf{w}|P|\mathbf{s}\rangle+\langle \mathbf{w}|H|\mathbf{s}\rangle=0,
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where $\omega$ is the eigenfrequency, $\mathbf{s}$ the eigenfunction, and $\mathbf{w}$ is an arbitrary test function.
When the material parameters $\rho$, and $A_{ijkl}$ are spherically symmetric\DIFdelbegin \footnote{\DIFdel{The most general elastic tensor that is 
consistent with spherical symmetry is }\textbf{\DIFdel{transversely isotropic}}
%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{with a radial symmetry axis. There are then }\textbf{\DIFdel{five elastic modulii}}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{,
each of which can vary with radius.}}%DIFAUXCMD
\addtocounter{footnote}{-1}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{,}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{,}\footnote{\DIFadd{The most general elastic tensor that is 
consistent with spherical symmetry is }\textbf{\DIFadd{transversely isotropic}}
\DIFadd{with a radial symmetry axis. There are then }\textbf{\DIFadd{five elastic moduli}}\DIFadd{,
each of which can vary with radius.}} \DIFaddend it can be shown that the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{possible eigenfunctions take }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{eigenfunctions are of }\DIFaddend two possible types known as 
\textbf{toroidal} and \textbf{spheroidal modes}. This can be shown either through a direct but lengthy calculation using 
spherical harmonics, or using representation theory for the rotation group. For reasons of both time and difficulty, we 
here just summarise the eventual results.

First there are toroidal modes which have eigenfunctions of the form
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\mathbf{s}(r,\theta,\varphi)=W(r)\mathbf{C}_{lm}(\theta,\varphi),
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:13}
\mathbf{s}(r,\theta,\varphi)=W(r)\mathbf{C}_{lm}(\theta,\varphi),
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
with $W$ a function of radius that depends on $l$ but not \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{m}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$m$}\DIFaddend . 
The corresponding eigenfrequency is also independent of $m$, and hence such modes lie in $(2l+1)$-fold degenerate eigenspaces. 
It can be shown that toroidal modes are associated with no perturbation to the gravitational potential, and also that their 
eigenfunctions are non-zero only in solid parts of the earth model.

The second type of eigenfunction is the spheroidal modes that take the form
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\mathbf{s}(r,\theta,\varphi)=U(r)\mathbf{A}_{lm}(\theta,\varphi)+V(r)\mathbf{B}_{lm}(\theta,\varphi),
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:14}
\mathbf{s}(r,\theta,\varphi)=U(r)\mathbf{A}_{lm}(\theta,\varphi)+V(r)\mathbf{B}_{lm}(\theta,\varphi),
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where $U$ and $V$ are functions of radius that depend on $l$ but not $m$.
Again, such an eigenfunction lies in a $(2l+1)$-fold degenerate eigenspace. Spheroidal modes 
are associated with gravitational perturbations, and their eigenfunctions can be non-zero in 
both solid and fluid regions of the earth model.

Let us consider toroidal modes further. If we put eq.\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{(13}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{~(\ref{eq:13}}\DIFaddend ) into the kinetic energy form, then due to the orthogonality 
of vector spherical harmonics, we can only get a non-zero contribution from the part of the test function proportional 
to $\mathbf{C}_{lm}$. The same turns out to be true for the potential energy form, and hence it is sufficient 
to consider test functions
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\mathbf{w}(r,\theta,\varphi)=W'(r)\mathbf{C}_{lm}(\theta,\varphi)
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:15}
\mathbf{w}(r,\theta,\varphi)=W'(r)\mathbf{C}_{lm}(\theta,\varphi).
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
Using eq.\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{(8}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{~(\ref{eq:10}}\DIFaddend ) it is then easy to obtain
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\langle \mathbf{w}|P|\mathbf{s}\rangle=\zeta^{2}\int_{0}^{b}\rho W'^{*}Wr^{2}\dd r,
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:16}
\langle \mathbf{w}|P|\mathbf{s}\rangle=\zeta^{2}\int_{0}^{b}\rho W'^{*}Wr^{2}\dd r,
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where we have set $\zeta=\sqrt{l(l+1)}$ and have assumed the earth model is entirely solid. 
In a similar manner, it can be shown that
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\langle \mathbf{w}|H|\mathbf{s}\rangle=\zeta^{2}\int_{0}^{b}\mu(r\frac{\ddns W'^{*}}{\ddns r}-W'^{*})(r\frac{\ddns W}{\ddns r}-W)\dd r+\zeta^{2}(\zeta^{2}-2)\int_{0}^{b}\mu W'^{*}W\dd r.
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:17}
\langle \mathbf{w}|H|\mathbf{s}\rangle=\zeta^{2}\int_{0}^{b}\mu\left(r\frac{\ddns W'^{*}}{\ddns r}-W'^{*}\right)\left(r\frac{\ddns W}{\ddns r}-W\right)\dd r+\zeta^{2}(\zeta^{2}-2)\int_{0}^{b}\mu W'^{*}W\dd r.
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
Note that derivation of this result is not simple, as you need to know how to differentiate vector spherical harmonics. 
Nonetheless, the end result is that the eigenvalue problem for a toroidal mode of degree $l$ reduces to the form
\DIFdelbegin \begin{eqnarray*}
&\DIFdel{-\omega^{2}\int_{0}^{b}\rho W'^{*}Wr^{2}\dd r+\int_{0}^{b}\mu\left(r\frac{\ddns W'^{*}}{\ddns r}-W'^{*}\right)\left(r\frac{\ddns W}{\ddns r}-W\right)\dd r\nonumber }\\ &\DIFdel{+(\zeta^{2}-2)\int_{0}^{b}\mu W'^{*}W\dd r=0 ,
}\end{eqnarray*}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{align}
&\DIFadd{-\omega^{2}\int_{0}^{b}\rho W'^{*}Wr^{2}\dd r+\int_{0}^{b}\mu\left(r\frac{\ddns W'^{*}}{\ddns r}-W'^{*}\right)\left(r\frac{\ddns W}{\ddns r}-W\right)\dd r\nonumber }\\ &\DIFadd{+(\zeta^{2}-2)\int_{0}^{b}\mu W'^{*}W\dd r=0,\label{eq:18}
}\end{align}\DIFaddend 
where $W$ is the radial eigenfunction, and $W'$ acts as the test function. Through a relatively simple calculation, 
it can be shown that this is an \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{ODE-eigenvalue problem of }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{ODE eigenvalue problem of }\DIFaddend \textbf{\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Sturm-Lioville }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Sturm--Liouville }\DIFaddend type} that many of you will have seen previously.
In the case of a homogeneous planet closed-form solutions can be obtained using \textbf{spherical Bessel functions}, though such 
a solution is of little practical use. More generally, the resulting ODE eigenvalue problem can be solved numerically, or the weak 
form directly converted into an algebraic eigenvalue problem using finite element methods. For our purposes, the key result is that 
for each degree $l\ne0$ there \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{exist }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{exists }\DIFaddend a countable infinity of toroidal modes, with these different solutions being labelled by an 
\textbf{overtone number}, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$n=0,1,2,...$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$n=0,1,2,\dots$}\DIFaddend , with $n=0$ being the \textbf{fundamental mode} and eigenfrequency then increasing with $n$.

For spheroidal modes a similar approach can be applied, with the eigenvalue problem reducing for each degree, $l$, 
to one involving the radial eigenfunctions $U$ and $V$. Solution of this latter problem is considerably more involved 
than that for toroidal modes, in part due to the need to model the associated gravitational potential perturbations. Nonetheless,
numerical solutions of this problem have been routinely determined for decades.

We now summarise the results of this section. Within a spherically symmetric earth model there are two families of normal 
modes: \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{toroidals }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{toroidal }\DIFaddend and spheroidal. We can introduce an index, $q$, to distinguish these with, say, $q=1$ meaning spheroidal, 
and $q=2$ toroidal\DIFdelbegin \footnote{\DIFdel{Along with further values if we need to distinguish toroidals in different solid parts of the 
model such as the mantle and inner core.}}%DIFAUXCMD
\addtocounter{footnote}{-1}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{.}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{.}\footnote{\DIFadd{Along with further values if we need to distinguish toroidals in different solid parts of the 
model such as the mantle and inner core.}} \DIFaddend Having fixed the mode type, $q$, we can then consider different degrees, $l$. 
For each such degree there is an infinite set of eigenfrequencies and radial eigenfunctions labelled by their overtone number, $n$.
Finally, we then have the order, $m$, of a mode, this labelling an orthogonal basis for the $(2l+1)$-fold degenerate eigenspace. 

In this manner, we see that \textbf{four integers} $(q,l,n,m)$ are sufficient to label all the eigenfunctions of a spherically symmetric earth 
model, while the corresponding eigenfrequencies depend only on $(q,l,n)$. As a useful shorthand, we can collapse the triplet \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$(q, l, n)$
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$(q,l,n)$
}\DIFaddend into a single index, $k$, and hence write $\omega_{k}$ for the eigenfrequencies and $|km\rangle$ for the eigenfunctions. 
Indeed, this is precisely the notation used in the last lecture. When needed, however, we can also write $|qlnm\rangle$ to 
explicitly characterise an eigenfunction. As a final bit of terminology, for each $(q,l,n)$ we call the eigenspace a \textbf{multiplet},
while individual elements of its basis, as labelled by $m$, are known as \textbf{singlets}. We might then, for example, talk about a 
degree two toroidal multiplet which is comprised of five singlets. A common labelling scheme for toroidal multiplets is to 
write ${}_{n}T_{l}$ for the multiplet of degree $l$ and overtone number $n$. Similarly, spheroidal multiplets are written ${}_{n}S_{l}$.

\subsection*{Splitting of a multiplet due to rotation and lateral heterogeneity}

Within a spherically symmetric earth model, the normal modes come in $(2l+1)$-fold degenerate multiplets, with $l$ the degree which is 
a non-negative integer. Such degeneracy is a direct consequence of the spherical symmetry of the problem, and analogous results are seen in other parts of physics. 
In particular, you know that the energy eigenspaces for the hydrogen atom are similarly degenerate, though note that here there is additional degeneracy associated 
with the ``hidden symmetry'' within central force problems due to the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Runge-Lenz }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Runge--Lenz }\DIFaddend vector. 

If such a spherically symmetric system undergoes an aspherical perturbation we expect that this degeneracy will be broken, with the singlets that
 comprise the multiplet splitting apart. This is precisely what happens in the \textbf{\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Zeeman-effect }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Zeeman effect }\DIFaddend for \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{a }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the }\DIFaddend hydrogen atom}, and we will apply very 
 similar mathematics to examine what rotation and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{laterally }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{lateral }\DIFaddend heterogeneities do to the free oscillations of the Earth.

We consider a spherically symmetric earth model along with the non-rotating eigenvalue problem
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{-\omega^{2}\langle \mathbf{w}|P_{0}|\mathbf{s}\rangle+\langle \mathbf{w}|H_{0}|\mathbf{s}\rangle=0.
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:19}
-\omega^{2}\langle \mathbf{w}|P_{0}|\mathbf{s}\rangle+\langle \mathbf{w}|H_{0}|\mathbf{s}\rangle=0,
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where we note that the subscript $0$ has been added to the kinetic and potential energy forms. A multiplet of 
this earth model is identified by the triplet \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{(q,l,n)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$(q,l,n)$}\DIFaddend , and we write $\omega_{k}$ and $|km\rangle$ for its 
eigenfrequency and eigenfunctions using the simplified notations discussed above. We then have, in particular, that
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{-\omega_{k}^{2}\langle \mathbf{w}|P_{0}|km\rangle+\langle \mathbf{w}|H_{0}|km\rangle=0,
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:20}

\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}

\subsection*{The diagonal sum rule and spherically symmetric density variations}
Within the first-order splitting calculations earlier, we \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{consider }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{considered }\DIFaddend a Hermitian eigenvalue problem of the form
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\frac{1}{2\omega_{k}}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}[-\omega_{k}^{2}\langle km'|P_{1}|km\rangle+\ii\omega_{k}\langle km'|W|km\rangle+\langle km'|H_{1}|km\rangle]a_{m}=\delta\omega a_{m'}
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:29}
\frac{1}{2\omega_{k}}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}[-\omega_{k}^{2}\langle km'|P_{1}|km\rangle+\ii\omega_{k}\langle km'|W|km\rangle+\langle km'|H_{1}|km\rangle]a_{m}=\delta\omega a_{m'}.
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
The perturbations to the singlet eigenfrequencies are equal to the eigenvalues of the matrix on the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{left hand }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{left-hand }\DIFaddend side. 
Based on so-called \textbf{selection rules} for the matrix elements\DIFdelbegin \footnote{\DIFdel{That are near identical to those arising in the quantum theory
of angular momentum}}%DIFAUXCMD
\addtocounter{footnote}{-1}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{,}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{,}\footnote{\DIFadd{These are nearly identical to those arising in the quantum theory
of angular momentum.}} \DIFaddend it can be shown that this matrix has \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{zero-trace}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{zero trace}\DIFaddend . Because the trace of a matrix is invariant under
similarity transforms, it follows that the singlet eigenfrequency perturbations sum to zero. 
This result is known as the \textbf{diagonal sum rule} and was obtained by \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Freeman Gilbert in }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Gilbert (}\DIFaddend 1971\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{and played an }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{) and played a }\DIFaddend key role in early studies of
Earth structure using observations of free \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{oscillation}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{oscillations}\DIFaddend . 

In the previous lecture we saw that eigenfrequencies can be measured
from the amplitude spectra of sufficiently long seismograms. The first such observations were made following the 1960 Great\footnote{``Great'' is a 
technical term used for especially large earthquakes\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{; }\DIFaddend it does not suggest that the earthquake was a good thing.} Chilean
earthquake which remains the largest event that has occurred during the instrumental period. By this time there existed good models 
of the Earth's spherically symmetric velocity structure obtained from travel time observations. There also existed plausible models 
of radial density variations that were built using knowledge of the Earth's mass, moments of inertia, and simple thermodynamic models of 
density variations in a homogeneous \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{solids }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{solid }\DIFaddend compressed under its own weight. As a consequence, the first spectra observed could be 
immediately compared to eigenfrequencies calculated in the different earth models available.

An example of this can be seen in Fig\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{3. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{.~\ref{fig:3}. }\DIFaddend Here two reasonably clear resonance peaks can be identified, with their centre frequencies measured. 
Also shown on the plot are the predicted eigenfrequencies from two different spherical earth models for the same multiplet. It is notable, 
firstly, that these predictions are in reasonable agreement with an observed eigenfrequency\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. But }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, but }\DIFaddend also that while in a spherical earth 
model we would expect only one eigenfrequency in this range, there \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{seems }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{seem }\DIFaddend to be at least two in the data. The latter fact is due to 
splitting associated with rotation and lateral heterogeneity. This fact was immediately recognised, and motivated George Backus 
and Freeman Gilbert to publish \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{in (1961) }\DIFdelend the first theoretical treatment \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(Backus \& Gilbert 1961) }\DIFaddend of the splitting of free oscillations due to rotation. 
Indeed, as we have seen, such calculations mirror almost exactly those known from quantum mechanics for decades at that point.

During these early days of seismology, the main emphasis was on better constraining the Earth's spherically averaged structure. 
With travel time observations this could be easily done. For many source-receiver pairs the travel time of different seismic phases 
could be measured. This information could then be reduced to plots of \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{travel-time }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{travel time }\DIFaddend against epicentral angle. Due to lateral heterogeneity, 
along with observational error, there was some scatter in these travel-time curves, but this could be averaged out. Note that the latter 
step is not rigorously justified, but it was at least easy to do. With free oscillations, however, the situation was quite different. 
What we observe are split eigenfrequencies of the singlets, but what we really want is the eigenfrequency of the associated multiplet 
prior to this splitting. Supposing that all the singlet frequencies for a split multiplet have been measured, Gilbert's diagonal sum 
rule provides an immediate answer. We need only take the arithmetic average of the split eigenfrequencies to obtain the degenerate 
eigenfrequency corresponding to the Earth's spherically averaged structure. For some low frequency and low degree multiplets it is 
indeed possible to identify all the singlet eigenfrequencies from a single spectrum. In other cases the singlet frequencies cannot 
be seen clearly in any one spectrum, but by combining observations from many different earthquakes and stations the singlet 
frequencies can be recovered using various signal processing methods.

Combining such observations with the diagonal sum rule, a large \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{data base }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{database }\DIFaddend of eigenfrequencies corresponding to the Earth's 
spherically averaged structure had been determined by the late 1970s. Taken along with travel time information, this allowed 
existing earth models to be substantially refined, and, in particular, for radial density variations to be found. The resulting 
density structure is shown in Fig.\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{4. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{~\ref{fig:4}. }\DIFaddend These observed densities can be compared with predictions made by mineral physicists subject 
to assumptions about the Earth's interior composition. For example, it has been shown that the density of the outer core is 
significantly lighter than expected if it were comprised of an \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{iron-nickel }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{iron--nickel }\DIFaddend alloy as is suggested \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{by }\DIFaddend observations of meteorites. 
This discrepancy is telling us something about how planets form and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{differentiation}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{differentiate}\DIFaddend . As yet the identity of this ``light element''
in the core is uncertain, with oxygen or silicon being perhaps the most likely candidates. But what is worth emphasising is that 
such discussions are only possible due to the highly accurate constraints on the interior structure provided by seismology.

\subsection*{Lateral density variations and mode coupling}

As noted in Lectures\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{18 and }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{~}\DIFaddend 19 \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and~20}\DIFaddend , recent work in seismology is focused on lateral heterogeneity in the mantle, this being done to 
better understand mantle convection. In Fig.\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{5 }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{~\ref{fig:5}, }\DIFaddend images from a range of S-wave velocity models are shown at different depths within 
the Earth, and a broad agreement can be seen. In particular, all models show at the base of the mantle two large regions with low 
shear wave velocity, one below the Pacific, and the other below Africa. 

On the assumption that these velocity variations reflect 
temperature variations, then we conclude that these so-called \textbf{Large Low Shear Velocity Provinces (LLSVPs)} are hotter than the 
surrounding mantle. If this is true, then we would also expect that these regions are lighter than the ambient mantle, and hence 
will experience an upwards buoyancy force. When LLSVPs were first identified in the early \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{90s }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{1990s }\DIFaddend such an interpretation was taken 
for granted, and they were known as ``super plumes''. A range of subsequent work, however, has brought into question this conclusion. 
For example, joint S- and P-wave tomographic models have shown that the LLSVPs seem \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{are }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{to be }\DIFaddend associated with a positive perturbation to 
the bulk sound speed
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\rho}}.
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:30}
\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\rho}}.
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
Note that this is not a speed of any elastic wave, but rather a parameter that is useful because it depends only on the bulk and 
not shear modulus. 

If the lateral variations are caused by temperature, then we would expect that the shear and bulk sound velocities 
would \textbf{positively correlate}. To see this, we assume that temperature variations are sufficiently small to allow us to write
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\delta\beta=\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial T}\delta T, \quad \delta\gamma = \frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial T}\delta T.
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:31}
\delta\beta=\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial T}\delta T, \quad \delta\gamma = \frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial T}\delta T,
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where $\delta T$ is the temperature perturbation. The relevant partial derivatives can be estimated from mineral physics, and are 
negative for all crystalline materials, and hence
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\frac{\delta\gamma}{\delta\beta}=\frac{\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial T}}{\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial T}}>0.
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:32}
\frac{\delta\gamma}{\delta\beta}=\frac{\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial T}}{\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial T}}>0,
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
as required. The fact that shear and bulk sound speeds seem to anti-correlate in the lower mantle suggests that temperature is not 
the only cause for these lateral variations. The other possible explanation is that \textbf{compositional variations} in the mantle play an 
important role in its dynamics. To see how this might reconcile the observations, we write $X$ for a compositional \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{variables}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{variable}\DIFaddend . The 
velocity perturbations can then be expressed in terms of temperature and composition in the form
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\delta\beta=\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial T}\delta T+\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial X}\delta X, \quad \delta\gamma=
\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial T}\delta T+\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial X}\delta X;
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:33}
\delta\beta=\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial T}\delta T+\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial X}\delta X, \quad \delta\gamma=
\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial T}\delta T+\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial X}\delta X,
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
from which it is clear that the ratio $\frac{\delta\gamma}{\delta\beta}$ need not always be positive. If it is accepted that 
significant compositional variations exist in the lower mantle, then we cannot \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{determined }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{determine }\DIFaddend from the tomographic \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{students }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{studies }\DIFaddend discussed 
whether LLSVPs are \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{be }\DIFdelend light or dense, and hence the role they play in mantle dynamics. To address this latter point what 
is needed are direct estimates of their density, and it \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{is }\DIFaddend free oscillations that have the potential to provide this information.

The first study of lateral density variations in the lower mantle using free oscillation data was undertaken in the late \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{90s }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{1990s }\DIFaddend by 
Miaki Ishii and Jeroen Tromp\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, }\DIFaddend then both at Harvard \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{and Miaki }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(Ishii being }\DIFaddend a PhD student \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{at the time; Ishii \& Tromp 1999)}\DIFaddend . This study was based on a slight elaboration \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{of }\DIFaddend the first-order splitting theory 
discussed earlier in the lecture, and the resulting models showed that LLSVPs are denser than the surrounding mantle. This is consistent 
with the presence of compositional variations, and hence leads to interesting questions about what these variations are, and how they were 
produced. Do, for example, LLSVPs represent a \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{"slab graveyard" }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{``slab graveyard'' }\DIFaddend comprised of compositionally distinct material accumulated at the base of 
the mantle through subduction\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{? }\DIFaddend Or are they instead ancient iron-enriched features produced as the Earth differentiated into a mantle 
and core? Such questions continue to engage a wide range of Earth \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Scientists}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{scientists}\DIFaddend .

But the density of LLSVPs is actually not a settled issue. 
This is because later work has shown that the simple first-order degenerate perturbation theory applied is not sufficiently accurate to 
model the effect of lateral density variations. Explicit within this theory is the assumption that on the addition of laterally varying structure
distinct multiplets do not interact with each other. This is not true, with the resulting errors being especially significant 
for nearly degenerate multiplets. A better approach is to use \textbf{normal mode coupling theory}, this being based on the expansion of the eigenfunctions 
in a 3D earth model in the form
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\mathbf{s}=\sum_{k'm'}\langle k'm'|P_{0}|\mathbf{s}\rangle|k'm'\rangle ,
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:34}
\mathbf{s}=\sum_{k'm'}\langle k'm'|P_{0}|\mathbf{s}\rangle|k'm'\rangle,
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where $|km\rangle$ are eigenfunctions in a spherical reference model. Putting such an expansion into the rotating eigenvalue problem we arrive at
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{\sum_{k'm'}[-\omega^{2}\langle km|P|k'm'\rangle+i\omega\langle km|W|k'm'\rangle+\langle km|H|k'm'\rangle]\langle k'm'|P_{0}|\mathbf{s}\rangle=0 ,
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:35}
\sum_{k'm'}[-\omega^{2}\langle km|P|k'm'\rangle+\ii\omega\langle km|W|k'm'\rangle+\langle km|H|k'm'\rangle]\langle k'm'|P_{0}|\mathbf{s}\rangle=0,
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
for all \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{(k,m)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$(k,m)$}\DIFaddend . This is a non-linear eigenvalue problem for the expansion coefficients $\langle k'm'|P_{0}|\mathbf{s}\rangle$ relative 
to the chosen basis. Such an equation cannot be solved directly because the matrices and vectors involved are infinite-dimensional. But it is 
possible to truncate the basis so as to obtain a finite-dimensional problem that can be solved numerically, and by taking sufficiently many 
terms in the expansion the results can be made as accurate as desired.

The computational task here is fairly substantial. It has only been in the past decade or so that such methods have become viable, 
while work continues to make these calculations efficient enough to use routinely within inversions. Nevertheless, 
through such calculations it has been shown conclusively that the earlier first-order approaches were inadequate, and 
hence the results of Ishii and Tromp cannot be taken at face value. This being said, a more recent but related study was
led by Harriet Lau using observations of solid Earth tides \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(Lau et al.\ 2017)}\DIFaddend . In this case sophisticated and accurate numerical modelling 
was done, with the result of this analysis still supporting the idea that LLSVPs are dense stable features 
at the base of the mantle.
